The six numbers

These fully define the size, shape, tilt, and orientation of the orbit.

How tilted the orbit is compared to Earth's equator. 0° hugs the equator; 90° passes over the poles. Higher inclination = more of the globe gets overflown.

RAAN is the ascending node — same setting, two names. It swings the entire orbital ring around Earth's axis, like spinning a hula-hoop, fixed against the distant stars rather than against the ground below.

How stretched the oval is. 0 is a perfect circle; closer to 1 is a long ellipse that swings from very close to Earth to very far away.

Once the ring is tilted and aimed (inclination + RAAN), this says where along that ring the closest point to Earth sits.

Where the satellite starts along its orbit, as an angle rather than a clock time. 0° places it right at perigee.

Orbits completed per day. Low, fast orbits near Earth do many per day (the ISS: about 15.5); far, slow orbits do few (geostationary: exactly 1). Altitude and period below are calculated from this, not set directly — that's how real TLEs work too.

Background

What's actually in a TLE?

A Two-Line Element set is a compact, fixed-width text format — two lines of about 69 characters each — that organizations like NORAD publish so anyone can predict where a satellite will be. It encodes exactly the six numbers above, plus a timestamp called the epoch (the moment the numbers are valid for — orbits drift, so TLEs go stale after days to weeks) and a drag term called BSTAR that describes how much atmospheric drag is slowing the satellite down over time.

This simulator generates a realistic-looking TLE from your sliders (see the box above) but treats BSTAR as zero and epoch as a fixed snapshot, so orbits here never decay.

How accurate is the physics here?

Positions are computed with real two-body orbital mechanics: your six numbers are converted to a size and shape (via Kepler's equation), then rotated into 3D space and spun beneath a rotating Earth to build the ground track. That's genuine orbital math, not a canned animation.

What's left out: real satellite trackers use a model called SGP4, which adds corrections for Earth's slightly flattened shape, atmospheric drag, and other small forces. Those cause real orbits to slowly precess and decay — effects you won't see here.

Quick reference

Perigee — closest point to Earth in the orbit. Apogee — farthest point. LEO (low Earth orbit) — roughly 160–2,000 km up, where the ISS and most imaging satellites fly. MEO — GPS-type orbits. GEO (geostationary) — about 35,786 km up, where the orbit period matches Earth's rotation so the satellite appears to hang over one spot.
