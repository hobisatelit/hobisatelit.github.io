About this comparison tool

Is this real satellite tracking?

Yes, for most satellites. Positions now come from SGP4 — the actual propagator NORAD's TLEs are built for, the same model used by Celestrak, N2YO, and most tracking software. It's implemented here from scratch and checked against a published reference case (a real ISS TLE propagated to a known time), matching the official result to within a few meters.

BSTAR is a genuine SGP4 input, not a bolt-on effect — realistic drag values now produce realistic decay (an ISS-like BSTAR loses roughly a tenth of a kilometer of altitude per day here, in line with real-world figures) because it falls straight out of the same equations NORAD uses.

Where does it fall short of full accuracy?

SGP4 actually splits into two models: itself for satellites with orbital periods under 225 minutes (almost all LEO objects — ISS, Starlink, most imaging and CubeSats), and a companion deep-space model, SDP4, for anything slower (GPS, geostationary, Molniya, and similar). SDP4 adds lunar and solar gravity plus resonance corrections that near-earth SGP4 doesn't include.

This tool implements SGP4 only. Deep-space satellites are still propagated with real J2/J4 gravity and drag physics — far closer than simple two-body math — but without those lunar-solar and resonance terms, so expect growing drift over multi-day predictions for that class of orbit. Cards for those satellites are marked "deep-space regime" in their stats.

What's being compared, exactly?

Each satellite keeps its own real TLE epoch — nothing is fast-forwarded or approximated to line elements up. The globe, ground track, and current-time control all share one clock, so what you're seeing is genuinely how these orbits relate to each other at that real moment, computed independently for each satellite and never chained together as a single animation.

Why are there two orbit rings on the globe?

The faint ring is fixed — the orbital plane exactly as it was at epoch, for reference. The bright ring is live: it uses the plane's actual current RAAN, argument of perigee, semi-major axis, and eccentricity, all pulled straight out of SGP4's perturbation math at whatever moment the current-time field is set to.

Play forward (especially sped up) and watch the bright ring swing away from the faint one — that's real nodal regression, the westward drift of where an orbit crosses the equator caused by Earth's slight equatorial bulge (J2). A satellite's inclination barely drifts this way, so it's carried over unchanged, but RAAN and argument of perigee can shift by several degrees a day for a typical LEO orbit. Each satellite's card also shows its RAAN drift since epoch as a number.
